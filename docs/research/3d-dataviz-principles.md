# Under what conditions is 3D visualization (rather than 2D) the appropriate choice for scientific, engineering, and information visualization, and what perceptual and design principles govern its effective use? Specifically: (1) when do the data's inherent dimensionality or spatial structure justify 3D over 2D or multiple coordinated 2D views; (2) how should depth cues, occlusion, and perspective/projection distortion be managed to avoid corrupting quantitative judgments; (3) how should color and colormaps be chosen for shaded 3D surfaces to avoid confounding lighting/shading with data-encoded color; (4) what are best practices for encoding data variables into position, scale, color, and motion in 3D scenes; (5) what are the established techniques and tradeoffs for volume rendering and isosurface extraction; (6) how should point clouds and meshes be visualized and rendered at scale (large N, real-time or interactive constraints); and (7) what do the visualization research literature's major critiques (e.g., of 3D bar/pie charts, chart-junk, unjustified 3D) and guidelines conclude about when 3D helps versus harms comprehension and comparison accuracy?

## 2. When Data Dimensionality or Spatial Structure Justifies 3D
Empirical comparisons converge on a task-dependent split: 3D displays support **shape understanding, object identification, and approximate spatial layout**, while 2D (or multiple coordinated 2D views) support **precise relative-position, distance, and magnitude judgments** [s61][s62][s68]. This has been demonstrated directly in shape-vs-relative-position tasks [s61][s68] and reinforced by broader reviews of 2D/3D presentation of spatial data [s49] and task-based effectiveness studies of basic visualizations [s3][s12]. Genuinely volumetric or spatial data — medical imaging, CFD, geological structure, terrain, molecular geometry, physical simulation, or navigable 3D environments — justify 3D when the task requires understanding containment, connectivity, trajectories, or surfaces [s61][s68]; mobile 3D map navigation is one documented application domain [s10]. Coordinated 2D views (linked slices, maps plus profiles, scatterplot matrices) are an established alternative that preserves measurement accuracy while relating multiple representations [s71][s74], and combined 2D+3D interfaces have been studied directly for orientation and relative-position tasks [s64]. The practical test proposed in this literature: if removing the depth axis removes a meaningful data relationship, 3D is justified; if it only removes decoration, prefer 2D [s58][s60].

## 3. Managing Depth Cues, Occlusion, and Projection Distortion
Depth in a 3D display is not a directly measurable screen quantity; it must be reconstructed from multiple, often conflicting cues — occlusion order, perspective size change, cast shadows, shading, texture gradients, stereo, motion parallax, and interactive rotation [s64][s69]. Occlusion specifically has been studied as a source of both information loss and depth-ordering error: dedicated work on 3D occlusion management and causality visualization [s9] and on depth discrimination from occlusion in cluttered 3D scenes [s73] documents that heavy occlusion hides data and privileges whatever is frontmost. Mitigations reported across this literature include viewpoint changes, clipping/cross-sections, cutaways, selective transparency, depth peeling, focus-plus-context, and temporary isolation of selected objects [s9][s73]. Perspective projection introduces foreshortening — equal data distances do not map to equal screen distances — which is a documented mechanism behind reduced accuracy of position/magnitude judgments in 3D [s68]; orthographic or weak-perspective projection, aligned axes, reference planes, and drop lines are the standard countermeasures. Perceptually motivated treatments of 3D displays more broadly discuss how these cues interact and can be exploited or mismanaged [s43][s69].

## 4. Color and Colormaps for Shaded 3D Surfaces
Modeland's dedicated color-map guidance for scientific visualization is explicit that shading-driven luminance changes (communicating surface normals and curvature) and data-driven color changes must be kept visually distinguishable, recommending colormaps whose luminance stays high enough not to be confused with shading [s31]. This is reinforced by general critiques of poor colormap practice — notably the case against rainbow-style maps for quantitative data because uneven perceptual lightness creates false boundaries [s39] — and by perceptually uniform color-map guidance intended to avoid this confound [s35][s36]. Lightness constancy research on surface visualization specifically addresses how viewers separate illumination-driven from data-driven lightness variation on shaded surfaces [s42]. Library/reference guidance on scientific visualization color choices reiterates checking maps under color-vision deficiency and providing calibrated legends [s37].

## 5. Encoding Variables into Position, Scale, Color, and Motion
Visual-encoding-principles teaching material and graphical-perception reviews rank encoding channels by judgment accuracy, placing common-scale position and length above area/volume, and those above color/shading [s46][s48][s53]. This accuracy ordering originates in the classical graphical-perception experimental program [s47][s51][s52][s54][s56][s57] and has been revisited in later reviews and crowdsourced replications [s13][s51]. Perceptual-accuracy comparisons across visual and tactile encodings extend this hierarchy empirically [s48]. Practices for 3D-specific dimensions (multivariate 3D scenes) are treated in dedicated work on 3D visualization of multivariate data [s65], which is consistent with, but adds detail beyond, the general 2D encoding hierarchy. Motion/animation as an encoding channel for time or process is not covered by a dedicated source in this set beyond the general framing already summarized; treat specific animation-design claims (e.g., about change blindness) as **unsupported by the provided sources** rather than citable.

## 6. Volume Rendering and Isosurface Extraction: Techniques and Tradeoffs
Direct volume rendering (DVR) — ray casting, splatting, shear-warp, texture-slicing — composites transfer-function-mapped voxel samples along view rays and is documented across multiple surveys and course materials [s17][s18][s21][s27][s28][s30]. Its central design difficulty is transfer-function specification; dedicated work directly compares transfer-function approaches (the "transfer function bake-off") [s19] and surveys transfer-function design methods [s23]. DVR shows multiple materials and fuzzy/semi-transparent boundaries without prior geometry extraction, at higher computational and perceptual cost, including compositing-order and clutter issues [s22][s24][s29]. Isosurface extraction (e.g., Marching Cubes) converts a scalar field to a polygonal mesh for a chosen threshold, yielding a crisp, fast-to-render, easy-to-light surface but only for the selected level(s), with sensitivity to threshold and noise [s16][s18][s20][s25]. Scalar-field visualization surveys treat both families as complementary rather than as a strict either/or choice [s22][s29]. Medical-imaging-specific volume-rendering discussion (including GPU implementations) is covered separately [s26].

## 7. Point Clouds and Meshes at Scale
Interactive exploration of large point clouds is addressed directly, with spatial-indexing structures (octrees), level-of-detail, culling, and progressive/streaming rendering as the standard toolkit [s38]. Simultaneous LOD generation and rendering for point clouds is a specific documented technique supporting real-time interaction while data continue loading [s41]. Virtualized point-cloud rendering for very large datasets is covered as a distinct approach to managing scale [s44]. The tradeoff pattern documented across these sources is fidelity versus interactive latency: aggressive LOD/decimation risks losing narrow features or rare points, while insufficient LOD breaks real-time interaction. Mesh-specific rendering-at-scale practices (decimation, indexed buffers, out-of-core streaming) are not covered by a dedicated source in this set beyond general graphics-pipeline framing implicit in the point-cloud/volume-rendering sources; treat mesh-specific claims beyond what point-cloud sources generalize to as **unsupported**.

## 8. Major Critiques: 3D Bar/Pie Charts, Chart-Junk, and When 3D Helps vs. Harms
The strongest, most specific empirical result in this evidence set is a controlled comparison of bar charts, pie charts, and tables with and without a "gratuitous" third dimension: adding 3D had no accuracy benefit for bar charts and a small but significant negative effect for pie charts [s5]. A related study of extraneous information in graph comprehension similarly reports reduced accuracy with 3D bar/pie presentation, including foreground-slice prominence effects that obscure other data [s2]. A more recent empirical evaluation of three-dimensional pie charts specifically revisits this question [s8]. Eye-tracking-based work on visual attention and comprehension across chart types [s11] and a broader eye-tracking scoping review of statistical graphics research [s6] provide additional (though not fully detailed here) empirical grounding for how 3D affects visual search and reading strategy. "Useful junk" research on visual embellishment complicates a purely negative reading of decoration, finding some embellishments aid memorability without necessarily harming comprehension [s60] — this is the clearest point of tension in the evidence (see Section 9). Tufte-style chart-junk critique, as summarized in secondary teaching material, treats gratuitous 3D as a paradigm case of non-data ink that should be removed unless depth encodes a real variable [s58].

## 9. Where the Sources Agree and Disagree
**Agreement:** All the task-comparison sources agree that 3D is not superior for precise quantitative comparison and that unjustified 3D (decorative depth, gratuitous perspective) tends to reduce or fail to improve accuracy for standard statistical charts [s2][s5][s61][s68]. All colormap sources agree that rainbow-style, non-perceptually-uniform maps are a documented hazard for quantitative reading, on 2D or 3D surfaces alike [s31][s39].
**Disagreement / tension:**
- The RAND study finds a null effect of 3D on bar-chart accuracy but a negative effect on pie-chart accuracy [s5], while the extraneous-information study reports negative effects more broadly across bar and pie presentations [s2]. The evidence does not resolve whether 3D bar charts are reliably harmless or whether harm is masked by task/design differences between studies — a later 3D-pie-chart-specific study [s8] would be needed to adjudicate the pie-chart magnitude question but its findings are not detailed in the evidence provided.
- The "useful junk" work [s60] reports that visual embellishment (which overlaps with what chart-junk critiques condemn) can aid memorability and engagement even when it does not aid — or even while modestly hurting — immediate reading accuracy. This sits in tension with the chart-junk framing [s58] that treats non-data ink as an unqualified negative; the sources do not adjudicate which goal (accuracy vs. memorability/engagement) should dominate design choice, and the provided evidence does not indicate they were reconciled.
- Sources describing 3D as beneficial for shape/spatial tasks [s61][s68] and sources documenting occlusion/depth-cue costs [s9][s73] are not in tension so much as describing different task regimes, but the evidence does not quantify the crossover point (how much occlusion or distortion must occur before a shape-understanding advantage is erased) — this remains a gap, not a resolved disagreement.

## 10. What Remains Unsettled and What Would Settle It
- **Magnitude of 3D pie/bar-chart harm across replications:** the RAND [s5] and extraneous-information [s2] studies differ in emphasis; a systematic replication across chart types, sample sizes, and task framings (comparison vs. estimation vs. ranking) would settle whether 3D harm is chart-type-specific or general.
- **Point at which embellishment/chart-junk shifts from beneficial (memorability) to harmful (accuracy):** the tension between [s60] and Tufte-style critique [s58] is not resolved by the provided sources; controlled studies varying embellishment type and measuring both immediate accuracy and delayed recall would be needed.
- **Mesh-rendering-at-scale best practices** are not directly documented in this source set (only point-cloud rendering is); a dedicated survey of large-mesh decimation/streaming tradeoffs would be needed to answer sub-question 6 fully for meshes.
- **Motion/animation encoding guidance** is not covered by a dedicated source here; this sub-question (part of sub-question 4) is currently **unsupported by the provided evidence** and should not be treated as settled.
- **Quantified occlusion/distortion thresholds** for when 3D's shape-understanding advantage is overtaken by depth-cue cost are not established in the provided sources; controlled psychophysical studies varying occlusion density and projection type against task accuracy would settle this.
